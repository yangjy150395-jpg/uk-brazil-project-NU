Wind Turbine Blade Thermography: Key Questions

A practical introduction to how thermography can reveal hidden damage in composite wind turbine blades, and why real blade geometry makes the inspection problem challenging.

Why these questions matter

Wind turbine blades are large, curved composite structures that operate under cyclic loading and harsh environmental conditions. Many important defects develop below the surface, so inspection methods must do more than identify visible damage. Active thermography uses a controlled thermal stimulus and an infrared camera to observe how heat moves through the blade. Subsurface defects disturb that heat flow and can therefore create measurable thermal signatures. The questions below explain the basic physics, practical limitations, and research challenges behind this approach.

Q1

What types of damage can occur in wind turbine blades?

Composite blades can develop delamination between laminate layers, adhesive or bond-line debonding, impact-related damage, cracks, voids, and other local discontinuities. Some of these defects can remain largely invisible at the surface while still affecting structural integrity.

Q2

How can thermography detect a defect that is below the surface?

In active thermography, the inspected region is heated in a controlled way and an infrared camera records the surface temperature as it changes with time. Heat normally diffuses through sound material in a characteristic way. A subsurface defect changes the local thermal path, so the temperature above or around the defect can evolve differently from neighbouring sound regions.

Detection therefore depends on the complete transient thermal response, not simply on whether one pixel looks hotter or colder in a single frame.

Q3

What is the difference between passive and active thermography?

Passive thermography observes naturally occurring temperature differences, for example those created by operating or environmental conditions. Active thermography deliberately introduces an excitation, such as optical, electromagnetic, or another controlled heat source, and analyses the resulting thermal transient.

Active methods are particularly useful when repeatable inspection conditions and defect-sensitive thermal responses are required.

Q4

Why are curved wind turbine blade surfaces difficult to inspect?

Blade curvature changes the distance and orientation between the surface, the excitation source, and the infrared camera. This can produce non-uniform heating, changes in apparent temperature, different viewing angles, and position-dependent measurement sensitivity. A thermal contrast caused by geometry can therefore resemble, hide, or distort the contrast caused by damage.

Q5

Why does fibre orientation matter in composite blades?

Composite laminates are anisotropic: heat and, in electrically conductive materials such as carbon-fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP), induced electrical current can travel differently along and across fibre directions. The stacking sequence and local fibre orientation therefore influence both the excitation field and the subsequent thermal response.

This means that two geometrically similar defects can produce different signatures when they are located in different laminate configurations.

Q6

How deep can thermography detect a defect?

There is no single depth limit that applies to every blade. Detectability depends on the material, thermal diffusivity, defect size and depth, excitation energy and duration, camera sensitivity, surface condition, signal-to-noise ratio, and the processing method used.

Deeper defects generally produce weaker and more spatially diffuse thermal signatures, which is one reason why reconstruction and inversion methods are important for quantitative inspection.

Q7

Where does Eddy Current Pulsed Thermography fit into blade inspection?

Eddy Current Pulsed Thermography (ECPT) combines electromagnetic induction with infrared imaging. In electrically conductive CFRP regions, induced currents generate transient Joule heating and damage can disturb both the current distribution and heat flow. Non-conductive glass-fibre reinforced polymer (GFRP) requires a different heating strategy or an additional conductive heating concept.

See What is ECPT? for a more detailed introduction and references.

Q8

Can thermography be used on real wind turbine blades?

Thermography is attractive for blade inspection because it is non-contact and can survey relatively large areas. Moving from laboratory coupons to real blades, however, introduces practical variations in curvature, access, surface emissivity, heating uniformity, environmental conditions, camera angle, and inspection distance.

Reliable field use therefore requires both appropriate excitation and analysis methods that can separate damage-related information from these measurement variations.

From measurement to interpretation

A simplified active-thermography inspection workflow.

1. Excite

Apply a controlled thermal or electromagnetic stimulus to the blade surface.

2. Record

Use an infrared camera to capture the surface-temperature evolution over time.

3. Reconstruct

Process the thermal sequence to reduce diffusion, geometry, and measurement effects.

4. Assess

Extract interpretable indicators for defect detection, localisation, and characterisation.

How this project addresses these challenges

Research directions linking blade physics to practical inspection.

Curved geometry

Study how blade curvature changes excitation, thermal transport, and measured surface temperature.

Anisotropy and fibre direction

Represent direction-dependent electrical and thermal behaviour in layered composite structures.

Improved excitation

Develop excitation strategies that provide stronger, more repeatable, and more informative thermal responses.

Reconstruction and inversion

Develop physics-aware data processing and learning methods to recover defect information from diffusive thermal measurements.

Key takeaways

  • Thermography detects damage indirectly by measuring how defects alter transient heat flow.
  • Blade curvature and composite anisotropy can change the thermal signature even when the defect itself is unchanged.
  • There is no universal defect-depth limit: inspection performance depends on the complete measurement system and material configuration.
  • The project combines improved excitation with reconstruction and inversion to make thermal data more reliable on realistic blade geometries.
